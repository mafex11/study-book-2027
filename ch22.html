Strategy to win the lab pre-application interview

When and how long: December to early February: the strategy for the official exam interview. Read once.

What this file is: what this interview is, what the lab is deciding, the five things you must show, the likely structure minute by minute, how to answer, the right tone, and the full path from here to the official exam in March 2027.

3. The five things you must show

3.1 Fit with the lab

What they check: that your topic fits their research and adds something new.

How to show it:

  • Name their papers, and one fact from each, in plain words:
    • Manifests (PROFES 2025) and Agent READMEs (TOSEM 2026) show what developers write in context files. Agent READMEs found Testing in 75.9% of files.
    • The EASE 2026 maintenance paper found humans made 83.21% of later commits on files that agents created. So agent mistakes cost humans time.
    • Voria et al. (ICSME 2026 registered report, with Kashiwa) plan to relate context file changes to code quality, as associations.
  • Say your one-line addition: "Your group studies context files from the repository side. I want to add a controlled experiment on single rules."
  • Mention Kashiwa's JST FOREST project ("Quality Assurance Foundation for AI-Driven Software Development", from Aug 2026) once, as a reason for fit. Do not claim you will be on it.
  • Be ready for: "If you want to do that, why do you need our lab?" [A]. Answer: "Because your lab already studies these files and agent code in real repositories. I need those mining and quality methods, and I would like to learn them from the people who wrote the closest papers to my question."

The trap: sounding like your study could be done anywhere, or sounding like you are correcting their papers.

3.2 Understanding of your own plan

What they check: that the proposal is yours and you can explain every choice.

How to show it:

  • Give the 30 second version without notes (see ../03_Research_Plan_Defense/01_proposal_explained_simply.md, section 0).
  • Explain Fig. 1 in 30 seconds, and draw it on paper if asked.
  • Know the arithmetic: 30 rules x 2 tasks x 5 versions x 2 agents x 3 runs = 1,800 runs at most.
  • Know why each version exists, especially the neutral sentence (it separates the rule's content from "just more text").
  • Know which parts are causal and which are associations: wording is tested directly; specificity, linter coverage and the real pull request study are associations.
  • Say the limits before they do: only script-checkable rules; 3 runs is a starting point; the pilot sets the final numbers.

The trap: reciting a memorised paragraph. If they interrupt, answer the question they asked, not the next line of your script.

3.4 Research potential and honesty

What they check: can you think like a researcher: careful claims, clear measures, open about what you do not know?

How to show it:

  • The BrandMemory story is your best proof. Tell it with the limit included: "One comparison is not a study, but it taught me to measure whether a model follows an instruction instead of assuming it."
  • Say "as far as I know" about novelty. Never "nobody has done this".
  • When you do not know, say so, then say how you would find out (section 5.3).
  • Fix the CV Japanese line yourself, early in the interview (you did not attach the corrected CV to the scheduling reply), and offer to send the corrected CV today.
  • Be clear that you have no research publication yet. Explain the 2023 IJIRAE paper honestly: it was a B.E. team project. In one sentence: "It is a review and concept paper from my B.E., published in a special issue of IJIRAE in June 2023; I was the second of four authors, Assistant Professor K. Radhika was the first author, and we ran no experiments of our own."
  • Show you read critically: "In Guardrails, of 18 rules, the three whose removal lowered the pass rate were all 'do not' rules. But they measured only pass rate, and the authors say their one individually significant rule would not survive a strict correction for multiple comparisons. So I treat it as a hint and test it with compliance."

The trap: defending every point. A good researcher agrees when the critic is right.

3.5 Commitment and English

What they check: are you serious, will you really come, and can you work in English?

How to show it:

  • Speak slowly and clearly. Your IELTS is 8.0 (Speaking 7.5). Clear beats fast.
  • Have one-line answers ready for the practical questions:
    • Start date: "I would prefer October 2027, because it lets me apply for the IPGP scholarship through your lab, but I can start in April 2027 if the lab prefers."
    • Your job: "I will leave Layerpath before I enrol and study full time." Decided: you will leave Layerpath before you enrol, so NAIST needs no employer documents. Do not mention a notice period.
    • Funding: "My first choice is the IPGP scholarship. If I am not selected, my savings from work and my family's support will cover the first year, and I will apply for JASSO support and NAIST's tuition exemption." See 00_intel_report.md, section 5.8.
    • Japanese: "I am a beginner, below N5. I am learning with the Genki I textbook and a daily app, about 30 minutes a day, and I want to reach N5 before I arrive and continue with NAIST's Japanese classes. My first CV said 'Limited Working' by mistake."
    • Long-term goal: "I want to decide about a PhD after my first real study. If my master's research goes well, I would like to continue to a PhD. Either way, I want to keep working on how developers can trust code that agents write."
  • Show that you already prepared: you read their papers, you know the exam dates, and you have a day-by-day maths plan for Strang and Lang that starts on Tue 27 Oct 2026.
  • Ask 2 or 3 good questions at the end (see 05_questions_to_ask_them.md).

The trap: vague answers on start date, job and money. Vague sounds like "not really coming".

5. How to answer

5.1 The basic rule: short answer, one example, stop

  1. Short answer first. One sentence that answers the exact question. Yes, no, a number, or your main point.
  2. One example or reason. One concrete thing: a paper, a step of your design, something you built.
  3. Stop. Let them ask the next question. Silence after your answer is normal.

Target: about 20 to 40 seconds per answer. Go past 60 seconds only when they ask you to explain more.

Example. Question: "Why do you need the neutral sentence version?"

  • Short answer: "To separate the content of the rule from just having more text in the file."
  • One example: "In Guardrails, random rules raised the pass rate as much as expert rules. So if removing a rule changes behaviour, I want to know whether it was the rule itself or just the length."
  • Stop.

5.2 When you did not understand the question

This is normal on video. Asking is much better than answering the wrong question. One past English-language applicant had to ask several times, and advises getting used to the faculty's English beforehand [A].

  • "Sorry, could you say that again, please?"
  • "Do you mean the controlled experiment or the real pull request study?"
  • "Just to check, are you asking about cost per run, or the total budget?"

5.3 How to say "I don't know"

Never bluff. A clear "I don't know, but here is how I would find out" shows research potential.

Type of question What to say
A fact you do not know "I don't know that number. I would check it in [the paper / the dataset / the pilot]."
A design problem you have not thought about "I have not thought about that yet. My first idea is [one idea]. I would test it in the pilot, and I would like to discuss it with my supervisor."
A paper you have not read "I have not read that paper yet. Could you tell me the title? I will read it this week." Then actually read it before the official exam. Past applicants were asked again about papers the lab mentioned [A].
A math or statistics question you cannot finish "I am not sure. Let me think aloud." Say what you know, step by step. If stuck: "Could you give me a hint?"
A question about the future (results, funding) "I don't know yet. My plan is [one line]."

Give yourself thinking time: "Let me think for a moment." A pause of 3 to 5 seconds is fine.

5.4 How to handle hard critiques

Use the same three steps every time (from 03_weak_points_and_honest_answers.md):

  1. Agree with the true part. "Yes, that is a real limit."
  2. Say what you will do about it. One or two concrete steps.
  3. Say what you will not claim. "So I will report it as an association." Or: "So my results only speak for this kind of rule."

Example. Critique: "Your rules are only script-checkable rules. Most important rules are about design."

  • "Yes, that is a real limit. I say it in the proposal."
  • "I chose them because a script gives the same answer every time and I can measure its accuracy. Later I could add some design rules with an LLM judge and a human check, reported separately."
  • "So my results will speak for checkable rules, not for design or process rules."

Do:

  • Stay calm and friendly. A hard question is a sign they take your plan seriously.
  • If they are right and you have no fix: "You are right. I would need to change the design. One option is [X]. Could I think about it and include it in my official proposal?"
  • If they suggest a change: "Thank you. That is a good idea," and write it down.
  • If you disagree, say it softly and with a reason: "I see the point. My worry is [reason]. Would [alternative] also work?"

Do not:

  • Start with "But".
  • Say a paper (theirs or anyone's) is wrong or weak.
  • Argue twice about the same point. Accept, note it, move on.

The one critique to prepare word by word: "Isn't this just the future work of our PROFES paper?"

"I read that section, and I was glad to see it. I came to this question from my own work, where some rules had no effect. My plan is narrower and different in one way: I test single real rules, one at a time, and I measure both whether agents follow each rule and how the code quality changes."

Do not start with "Yes". Do not say the lab's papers have not run it yet. Do not raise this future work section yourself. The same answer is C48 in ../03_Research_Plan_Defense/05_question_bank_with_model_answers.md and weak point 8 in ../03_Research_Plan_Defense/03_weak_points_and_honest_answers.md.

6. Tone for a Japanese academic setting

This is general etiquette advice, not from a NAIST source. It also works for an international faculty panel.

Politeness

  • Use titles and family names: "Professor Iida", "Dr. Reid", "Dr. Ji", "Dr. Kashiwa" (or "Professor" for each). Do not use first names unless they invite you to.
  • In emails: "Dear Prof. Iida", "Dear Dr. Reid". Sign with your full name and Reception No. 2027M3055.
  • Keep everyone they copied in your replies (for example sdlab-contact@is.naist.jp). Do not drop or add people.
  • Do not interrupt. Video has a small delay, so wait one second after they finish before you speak.
  • Nod and say "I see" or "Thank you" when they give advice. Write it down where they can see you do it.

Punctuality

  • Reply to their scheduling email within a few hours, the same day if possible.
  • Be ready 15 minutes before the call, and join 3 to 5 minutes early.
  • If anything goes wrong (internet, power), email them immediately with a one-line message and rejoin. See 03_day_of_checklist.md.

Thanking

  • Thank them at the start ("Thank you very much for taking the time to meet me today").
  • Thank them for advice during the call.
  • Thank them at the end, and send a short thank-you email the same day (04_email_templates.md).

Not overselling

  • Use careful words: "I think", "as far as I know", "my plan is", "I expect". Avoid "first ever", "revolutionary", "I am sure", "definitely".
  • Do not list CV numbers (calls per month, workspaces) unless asked. If asked, say how each number was measured, or say you are not sure.
  • Praise their work through accurate use, not compliments. "I read your study of 2,303 context files" is better than "your paper is amazing".
  • Never say your study is better than theirs. Say it "complements" or "adds a controlled experiment".
  • Do not ask "Will I be accepted?" Ask "What is the next step?"

Japanese words (optional)

  • The interview will be in English. Do not start in Japanese.
  • If it feels natural, you can end with one phrase: "Arigatou gozaimashita" (thank you very much). Only if you can say it comfortably. It is not needed.

7.2 What to do in each period

Before the lab interview (now):

  • Read your proposal out loud until you can explain every sentence.
  • Practise the 1 minute intro and the 2 minute pitch out loud. Record yourself once and listen.
  • Read the weak points file and practise the 12 most likely attacks.
  • Know your answers on start date, job, funding, Japanese learning plan, long-term goal and the 2023 paper (sections 3.5 and 5.6), and say them the same way every time.
  • Keep doing math every day. The official exam needs it, and the lab may ask a light question [INF].

Right after the lab interview:

  • Within 30 minutes, write down every question they asked, your answer, and what you would say better. These may come back in March [A].
  • Send the thank-you email the same day.
  • Read every paper they mentioned within a week.

November to January:

  • Math every day (Strang and Lang). The math part is 30 of 200 points, and the interview with the essay is 90 [V].
  • Update the proposal for the official application with the lab's feedback, if they gave any. Keep it consistent with what you said at the lab interview. A past applicant asked the examiners if he could explain an improved method, and they agreed [A].
  • Collect documents early: transcripts, degree certificate, IELTS PDF, photo.
  • Optional: the tiny pilot in ../03_Research_Plan_Defense/04_optional_mini_pilot.md, if you have time and budget.

February to March:

  • Apply on 8 to 10 Feb. Do not wait for the last evening.
  • Mock interviews without any notes. In the official exam no materials may be in sight, and the presentation is oral only [V].
  • Practise math on a sketchbook with a thick pen in front of a camera [V].

7.3 If the lab says no

  • NAIST says you may apply again to other professors until the pre-application form deadline (8 Dec 2026) [V]. Thank them politely in any case; the field is small.
  • Ask, politely, whether they have any advice for your plan. Do not argue with the decision.

8. The night before: the one-page summary

  1. Your story in one breath: "At Layerpath I saw models ignore rules we gave them as context. I measured it once and fixed it. Now I want to study it properly for coding agents, in the lab that already studies these files."
  2. Your plan in one sentence: "I test single real rules from CLAUDE.md and AGENTS.md files, by removing or rewording them one at a time, and I measure with scripts whether agents follow them and whether the code changes."
  3. Short answer, one example, stop.
  4. "I don't know yet. Here is how I would find out."
  5. Agree with the true part, say what you will do, say what you will not claim.
  6. Japanese: beginner, learning, the CV line was a mistake.
  7. Thank them at the start, during, at the end, and by email the same day.
