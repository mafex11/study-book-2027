Chapter 26 of 30

How to do oral math on camera with a sketchbook

When and how long: February to 10 March, step 4: how to do maths out loud in the exam.

Prepared 21 Oct 2026 for the official exam (online, Webex, in English). Facts marked (official) come from NAIST's own pages, the 2027 Guide or the official guideline video, as summarised in 00_syllabus_and_free_resources.md section 1. Facts marked (applicants) are anecdotal reports from past applicants. Everything else is my advice.

1. What they are scoring

  • Two problems on screen, one algebra and one analysis, about 12 minutes for both, no preview time and no choice (official). Several applicants report 10 minutes (applicants). Plan for about 5 minutes per problem.
  • They judge "not only whether you solved it, but the solving process, the clarity of your explanation, and your answers to related questions" (official, Japanese Q&A). You should "explain how you solve it while you solve it" (official).
  • Math is 30 of 200 points (official, may change).
  • Examiners interact. They give hints, point out sign errors ("is that right?"), and accept a clear plan when time runs out (applicants).

So the goal is not a silent perfect answer. The goal is that two examiners can follow your thinking at every moment, and see that you know the definitions and the method.

2. Your setup (prepare it once, test it every week)

Sketchbook and pen

  • A sketchbook "about A4 size" and a "thick pen, so that it is easy to see the answers" (official video).
  • My suggestion: a black marker with a tip of about 2 to 3 mm, plus two spares. Plain white matte paper (no shine under the lamp).
  • Use a new, blank sketchbook for the exam. The Guide says "make sure that no materials are in sight during the interview" (official). A practice page full of formulas could look like notes.
  • No eraser. If you make a mistake, cross it out with one clean line and keep going. Crossed-out work is fine; it shows your process.
  • My plan: I order two A4 sketchbooks (one for practice, one kept new for the exam) and three thick black markers online on Tue 27 Oct 2026, the day after the lab interview, so they arrive in the first week of the study plan.

Camera and room

  • Laptop or desktop with camera and microphone, or a smartphone on a tripod. Webex is used. Video and audio are both required (official video).
  • Quiet private room with no third person. The examiner may ask you to turn the camera to show the room (official video).
  • Light from the front (a window or lamp behind the camera). No strong light behind you.
  • Readability test (do it this week): write one line of a matrix and one integral at your normal exam size. Hold the page up at the distance you will use. Record a short video call (or a video with the same camera). On the recording, can you clearly read a minus sign, an exponent, a fraction bar and the letter λ? If not, write bigger.
  • My setup: the same computer, camera and microphone I use for the lab interview. I test it first in the Webex test meeting (webex.com/test-meeting.html), then in one call with a friend who tells me if every symbol on the sketchbook is readable. I repeat the friend call in the week before the 8 Mar 2027 connection test.

How big to write

  • About 6 to 10 lines per A4 page. That feels too big at first. It is right for a webcam.
  • One problem per page. Start a new page for each part if needed.

3. How to lay out a page

 (1) Algebra                          <- label
 A = [ 4 1 ]   eigenvalues?            <- copy the key data
     [ 2 3 ]
 Plan: det(A - λI) = 0                 <- one-line plan
 (4-λ)(3-λ) - 2 = λ² - 7λ + 10 = 0
 λ = 5, 2      check: 5+2 = 7 = trace ✓  5·2 = 10 = det ✓
 λ=5: (A-5I)x = 0  ->  x = (1, 1)
 λ=2: (A-2I)x = 0  ->  x = (1, -2)
 +------------------------------------------+
 | λ = 5, x = (1, 1);   λ = 2, x = (1, -2)  |   <- box the answer
 +------------------------------------------+

Rules:

  • Write a label ("(1) Algebra", "(a)", "(b)") at the top.
  • Copy only the data you need, then write "Plan:" and one line.
  • Write the check on the page (for example "5 + 2 = 7 = trace").
  • Box the final answer.
  • Big matrix brackets, with space between the entries.
  • Make easily confused symbols different: 1 and 7, 2 and z, x and ×, a long clear minus sign, λ, v and ν.

4. The six-step talk-aloud routine

Use this for every practice problem until it is automatic.

  1. Read and restate. Read the problem out loud, then say it in your own words: "So we need the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of this 2 by 2 matrix." If something is unclear, ask now: "Is A a 3 by 3 matrix?"
  2. Name the type. "This is a diagonalization problem." Naming it shows the examiners you recognise the topic.
  3. Say the plan in one sentence, before you write. "My plan is: eigenvalues from det(A - λI) = 0, then eigenvectors from the nullspace of A - λI, then S and Λ."
  4. Work and narrate. Say what you are doing and why. Say the key numbers as you write them. You do not need to say every small multiplication. Look up at the camera now and then.
  5. Check. One fast check per problem (see the list in section 9). Say it: "Let me check: the trace is 7 and 5 plus 2 is 7."
  6. Conclude and show. Say the answer as a full sentence, then hold the page up: "So the eigenvalues are 5 and 2, with eigenvectors (1, 1) and (1, -2)."

Holding the page up. The official video asks you to "hold the answer so that the examiners can see the solving procedures well". Hold it flat and still, filling most of the frame, for about 3 to 5 seconds. Do it after each part, and whenever they ask. Say "Can you see it clearly?" the first time.

Timing. For a 12 minute slot: about 5 minutes per problem, leaving 1 to 2 minutes for questions. If you notice you have spent 6 minutes on one problem, say where you are, state the remaining steps, and ask to move on. One 2024 applicant reports they could do the two problems in any order (applicants). If that is allowed, you can ask: "May I start with the analysis problem?"

5. Phrase bank (practise saying these, they buy you thinking time)

Starting

  • "Let me read the problem first." / "So the question asks for ..."
  • "This looks like a question about ... ."
  • "My plan is to ... . First I will ..., then ... ."

While working

  • "I subtract 2 times row 1 from row 2."
  • "Here I use the chain rule: the derivative of the outside, times the derivative of the inside."
  • "Since A is symmetric, I know the eigenvalues are real."
  • "I substitute u equals sin x, so du is cos x dx, and the limits change to 0 and 1."
  • "This is a 0 over 0 form, so I can use L'Hopital's rule, or a Taylor expansion."

Checking

  • "Let me check this by putting it back into the equation."
  • "Quick check: the sum of the eigenvalues should equal the trace."
  • "The answer should be positive, because the function is positive, and it is."

Correcting yourself

  • "Sorry, I made a sign error here. It should be minus 3."
  • "Wait, let me go back one line. I think I copied this wrongly."

Concluding

  • "So the answer is ... ."
  • "Let me show you the whole page." (hold it up)

Asking for clarification

  • "Could you please repeat the question?"
  • "Do you mean the eigenvalues of A, or of A^T A?"
  • "Should I give the answer for a general n, or is A 3 by 3?"

Asking for a hint

  • "I am not sure how to continue from here. Could I have a small hint?"
  • "I think I should use the definition of a positive definite matrix, but I do not remember all the tests. Could you remind me of the definition?"

When you do not know a follow-up

  • "I am not sure about the exact statement, but I know that ... ."
  • "I don't remember the name of that theorem. The idea is ... ."

6. When you are stuck: the recovery ladder

Never stay silent for more than about 10 to 15 seconds. One 2021 applicant sat silent for 5 minutes until the examiner said "you can tell us your thinking so far" (applicants). Silence gives them nothing to score. Climb this ladder instead, out loud:

  1. Say the definition of the key word, and write it. Often the definition is the method. "Positive definite means x^T A x > 0 for every nonzero x." Then the tests come back to you.
  2. Say what you notice about the object. Its size. Is it symmetric? Triangular? What is the trace, the determinant? Is the function even or odd? Where is it not defined?
  3. Try a small or special case. n = 2, a = 0, x = 0, a 2 by 2 example, the first two terms of a series.
  4. Work backwards from what is asked. "To find A^k, I need A = SΛS^-1. For that I need eigenvectors. For those I need eigenvalues."
  5. Switch method. Determinant instead of elimination. Taylor instead of L'Hopital. Parts instead of substitution. A picture instead of algebra.
  6. Ask for a hint, early. Applicants report that a hint costs little and keeps the conversation going (applicants, opinion). Use the phrases in section 5.
  7. If only heavy arithmetic is left, say the structure and ask to move on: "The rest is arithmetic. I would back-substitute from the bottom, z first. May I go on to part (b)?"

7. Showing partial progress

Because they score the process, make the process visible even when you cannot finish.

  • Always write the setup. The theorem or formula you will use, and what you will compute. That alone shows understanding.
  • If time runs out, say the remaining steps and the shape of the answer. Example: "Next I would normalise these three vectors and put them in Q. Then Q^T A Q is the diagonal matrix with 3, 0 and -3." A 2021 applicant who ran out of time explained the Gram-Schmidt plan, and the examiner then said "just find one such vector" (applicants).
  • If part (a) is stuck, ask: "May I assume the result of part (a) and do part (b)?"
  • Keep your intermediate results visible. If one number is wrong, the examiner can see that the method is right.
  • Give an answer even if you are unsure, and say how sure you are: "I get 1/6. I would double-check the sign of the cubic term."

8. When the examiner speaks

  • "Is that right?" or "Are you sure?" It usually means there is a mistake in the last line or two (applicants report sign errors being pointed out this way). Do not defend at once. Say "Let me check," and redo the last step slowly and out loud from the last line you are sure of. If you find the error: "Thank you, the sign was wrong. It should be ... ." If you do not find one: "I checked it again and I get the same, because ... . Please tell me if I missed something."
  • A hint. Thank them, repeat it in your own words, and use it immediately: "Thank you. So I should look at the columns. Then ... ."
  • A follow-up question. Answer short first (one sentence), then give one reason or example, then stop. Example: "Is every symmetric matrix diagonalizable?" "Yes. By the spectral theorem it has a full set of orthonormal eigenvectors, so A = QΛQ^T."
  • If you did not understand the English, ask them to repeat. That is normal and costs nothing.

9. Quick checks to say out loud

Situation Check
Solved Ax = b Put x back into one or two equations
Eigenvalues Sum = trace, product = det
Eigenvector Compute Ax for one of them
Inverse Multiply one row of A by one column of A^-1
Projection Error e is perpendicular to the subspace (A^T e = 0)
Gram-Schmidt All pairwise dot products are 0
Determinant Do it a second way (elimination vs cofactors), or test a special value
Derivative Plug in a simple x, or differentiate your antiderivative
Antiderivative Differentiate it and get the integrand back
Definite integral Sign and rough size (positive function, positive area; compare with a rectangle)
Limit Plug in a small number like x = 0.01 mentally, or use a second method
Max/min Compare with endpoint values
Series Compare with a known p-series or geometric series
Differential equation Put the solution back in and check the initial conditions

10. Common traps

Linear algebra

  • det(cA) = c^n det A, not c det A (LA16).
  • AB ≠ BA in general. (A + B)^2 = A^2 + AB + BA + B^2. The binomial theorem needs AB = BA (LA21).
  • (AB)^-1 = B^-1 A^-1 and (AB)^T = B^T A^T: the order reverses.
  • A basis of the column space uses the pivot columns of A, not of R (LA10).
  • An eigenvector is never the zero vector. Any nonzero multiple of an eigenvector is also fine as an answer.
  • A repeated eigenvalue does not guarantee diagonalizability ([a 1; 0 a] is not diagonalizable).
  • Singular values are not the eigenvalues (LA24: eigenvalues 3 and 5, singular values √45 and √5).
  • "Positive definite" is about symmetric matrices. Check symmetry first.
  • P = A(A^T A)^-1 A^T cannot be simplified to I unless A is square and invertible.
  • An "orthogonal matrix" has orthonormal columns (length 1), not only perpendicular ones.
  • In Gram-Schmidt, subtract projections onto the new orthogonal vectors, not onto the original ones.
  • In rank problems with a parameter, test every special value separately (LA8).
  • In the cofactor formula for the inverse, use the transpose of the cofactor matrix.
  • A linear map must send 0 to 0 (LA25).

Calculus

  • The chain rule's inner derivative: (sin 3x)' = 3 cos 3x, (e^(x^2))' = 2x e^(x^2).
  • d/dx (y^2) = 2y y' in implicit differentiation.
  • Forgetting + C, or the absolute value in ∫ dx/x = log |x|.
  • Substitution in a definite integral: change the limits too.
  • Radians, not degrees. sin x / x → 1 only in radians.
  • L'Hopital's rule only for 0/0 or ∞/∞, and you differentiate the top and the bottom separately (not the quotient rule).
  • Related rates: put in the numbers after differentiating.
  • Max and min on a closed interval: also check the endpoints.
  • f'(c) = 0 does not always mean a max or min (x^3 at 0). f''(c) = 0 does not always mean an inflection point (x^4 at 0).
  • Improper integrals: look for points inside the interval where the function blows up (1/x^2 on [-1, 1]).
  • Series: a_n → 0 is not enough (C22). The ratio test with limit 1 says nothing. Check power series endpoints separately (C23).
  • In Lang, log means the natural log. log 2 ≈ 0.693, log 3 ≈ 1.099, e ≈ 2.718.
  • Partial fractions: divide first if the top degree is at least the bottom degree.
  • Area between curves: find which curve is on top, and split where they cross.

11. A sample of good oral math (stuck, hint, recovery)

Problem on screen: "Is A = [2 1; 1 2] positive definite? Explain."

Me: "Let me read it. A is a symmetric 2 by 2 matrix, and the question is whether it is positive definite. I remember that positive definite means x transpose A x is positive for every nonzero x. Let me write that." (writes the definition) "One way is to compute x transpose A x directly. That is 2 x1 squared plus 2 x1 x2 plus 2 x2 squared." (pause, about 10 seconds) "I want to show this is always positive, but I am not sure how to see it immediately." Examiner: "Maybe there is a test with the entries?" Me: "Thank you. Yes, for a symmetric 2 by 2 matrix, the test is that the top-left entry is positive and the determinant is positive. The top-left entry is 2, which is positive. The determinant is 4 minus 1, which is 3, also positive. So A is positive definite. I can also confirm with the eigenvalues. The trace is 4 and the determinant is 3, so the eigenvalues are 3 and 1. Both are positive. And I can now finish my first idea by completing the square: 2 x1 squared plus 2 x1 x2 plus 2 x2 squared equals 2 (x1 plus x2 over 2) squared plus 3 over 2 x2 squared, which is positive unless x is zero. So the answer is yes, A is positive definite." (holds up the page)

Why this works: the definition is on the page, the pause is short, the hint is used at once, and the answer is confirmed two more ways.

12. Practice drills

  • Daily (10 minutes): one problem from 02_practice_problems_with_solutions.md, 5 minute timer, out loud, on the sketchbook. Then compare with the "Say it out loud" box.
  • Weekly mock (20 minutes): someone else picks one LA and one C problem at random. You get 12 minutes for both, including their follow-ups. Record it.
    • Partner instructions: read the problem aloud, stay quiet, once say "Is that right?" at a random moment (even if it is right), and ask one follow-up from the file at the end.
    • Examiner: a friend or colleague when one is free. Otherwise Claude plays the examiner in a chat session: it reads the problem, says "Is that right?" once, and asks one follow-up at the end. I record the mock either way.
  • Review your recording with these questions:
    • Did I say a plan within the first 30 seconds?
    • Was I silent for more than 15 seconds anywhere?
    • Can I read every symbol on the recording?
    • Did I do a check?
    • Did I hold the page up and say the answer as a sentence?
    • Did I finish within 5 to 6 minutes?
  • Vocabulary drill: read the word list at the end of 01_topic_checklist_and_key_formulas.md and explain each word in one sentence, out loud.

13. Exam-day checklist (math part)

  • New blank A4 sketchbook and 2 to 3 thick black markers.
  • Only items allowed by the official instructions on the desk. Read the instructions that come with the examination voucher. Until the voucher says otherwise, only the blank sketchbook, the markers and the device are on the desk. At the 8 Mar connection test, ask whether a glass of water is allowed.
  • Preliminary connection test (8 Mar 2027 for the Spring 3rd examination, per the 2027 Guide): use your real setup and ask whether the examiners can read your sketchbook.
  • 20 minutes before: say the one-page formula sheet aloud, and solve one easy problem (LA19 or C15) on scrap paper, then put the scrap paper away and out of sight.
  • At the start: greet them, listen to the instructions, and ask "May I start?" if it is not clear.
  • During: plan out loud, check, box, hold up. Ask for a hint before you freeze.
