Part 1: Before Monday (23 to 26 Oct)

Full script: from "hello" to the last slide

When and how long: Sunday: read it out loud three times, with the slides open. Monday 08:30: once more. About 6 minutes for the slides.

Monday 26 October 2026, 10:30 IST (14:00 JST), Google Meet with Dr. Zhenlan Ji and Dr. Brittany Reid. Words in [brackets] are actions. Do not say them.

Read this out loud at least three times today, so that tomorrow you are speaking it, not reading it.

Part A. Joining, before the slides

[Join at 10:25 IST. Camera on, mic on. Slides PDF already open in Preview.]

When they appear:

Good afternoon, Dr. Ji and Dr. Reid. Thank you very much for taking the time to meet me today.

If they ask "How are you?" or "Can you hear me?":

I am doing well, thank you. I can hear you clearly. I am joining from Bangalore, so it is morning here.

If they introduce themselves or the lab first, listen, then say:

Thank you. I have read your lab's papers on context files, so I am really glad to talk with you.

When they ask you to introduce yourself and your research:

Thank you. I prepared seven short slides, about six minutes. May I share my screen?

[In Meet: Present now, then "A window", then choose Preview. In Preview press Cmd + Shift + F.]

Can you see my slides?

[Wait for "yes".]

Part B. The slides (about 6 minutes)

Slide 1. Title (about 25 seconds)

My name is Sudhanshu Pandit. My research question is: which rules in a file like this CLAUDE.md do coding agents actually follow? For example, if it says "put new tests in the tests folder", does the agent really do it? And does keeping a rule change the quality of the code?

First, a little about me.

[Next slide]

Slide 2. About me (about 40 seconds)

I finished my B.E. in Computer Science and Engineering at Visvesvaraya Technological University in May 2026, and worked in software engineering roles during my studies. Since November 2025, I have been an AI engineer on the founding team at Layerpath, where I build systems on top of large language models.

On the right are three things I build. BrandMemory reads a company's writing rules from its website and gives them to a language model, so it writes in that company's style. Magic Editor is an AI assistant that edits product demos, and the user approves every edit. And Yuki, my side project, is a desktop AI agent for Mac and Windows.

I use Claude Code and Codex every day and maintain their context files. My research question started with BrandMemory.

[Next slide]

Slide 3. Where my question comes from (about 50 seconds)

When I measured BrandMemory's output, some rules had no effect, for two reasons. Some were cut off: they sat at the end of a long prompt and never reached the model. Others were overridden by a default house style.

I fixed both, then ran one A/B test on one product demo, without and with the rules. Contractions went from 6 to 0, and "you" and "your" from 15 to 1, which matches two of the rules.

That is one comparison, not a study. But it taught me to measure whether a model follows a rule, not assume it. Coding agents get their rules the same way, through CLAUDE.md and AGENTS.md, so I want to ask this question for them.

[Next slide]

Slide 4. What is known, and the gap (about 55 seconds)

Your lab's papers show what developers write in these files, and Agent READMEs also found fewer indentation violations after an indentation rule was added, in two repositories.

Gloaguen and colleagues found that a whole file does not generally raise task success and adds over 20 percent to cost on average; their traces suggest instructions are followed, but they did not test single rules. Zhang and colleagues removed 18 curated rules one at a time, but measured only task success. Harness-IF tests single rules, but on tasks written for the benchmark. And the registered report by Voria and colleagues, with Dr. Kashiwa, plans to relate file changes to code quality.

So, as far as I know, no study yet does these three things together: one real rule, real tasks from the same repositories, and scripts that measure both compliance and code quality.

[Next slide]

Slide 5. Three research questions (about 40 seconds)

RQ1 is the core: if I remove one real rule, do agents follow it less often on the same tasks?

RQ2 asks which kinds of rules work: negative or positive wording, specificity, and whether the linter already enforces the rule. Wording I test directly, because I reword the same rule. The other two differ between rules, so I report them as associations.

RQ3 asks whether keeping a rule changes code quality: tests, complexity and warnings. I compare file versions on the same task, not runs that happened to follow the rule, and I leave out any check that encodes the rule itself, so the result is not circular.

I call a rule effective if agents follow it more often when it is in the file than when it is removed.

[Next slide]

Slide 6. Design (about 60 seconds)

A task is a merged pull request whose own tests fail before and pass after. The agent starts just before it, with no git history, and the task text does not mention the rule.

Every task runs with five versions of the file: original, rule removed, rule reworded, a neutral sentence of the same length, and no file. So each task is its own control, and the neutral sentence separates the rule's content from just having more text. Claude Code and Codex get the same text, three runs per version, in a clean container.

A script measures what share of the applicable places follow the rule, and I check each script by hand on at least 20 examples. I also measure tests, complexity and warnings. With about 30 rules and 2 tasks each, that is at most 1,800 runs, about 1,300 to 1,900 dollars; a pilot on two repositories sets the final numbers.

Two limits: only rules a script can check, and one rule at a time cannot show how rules interact.

[Next slide]

Slide 7. Plan and expected results (about 40 seconds)

In Year 1: courses in software engineering and statistics, the checkers and harness, the pilot, then the main experiment, and I start the real pull request study. In Year 2: code quality, a first paper, and a small checker that developers rate. I expect three results: a public dataset of real, checkable rules, evidence on which kinds of rules work, and that small checker.

Why this lab: you study context files and agent code after it is merged, and your pull request study recommends a guideline file like CLAUDE.md. My study adds a controlled experiment on single rules: do agents then actually follow those rules? I also want to learn your methods for mining repositories and measuring quality. That is why I would really like to do this research in your lab.

Thank you very much. I would be glad to hear your questions and advice.

[Stop sharing.]

Part C. After the slides

Right after the last slide:

I will stop sharing so we can talk.

Useful lines during the questions:

  • Did not hear the question: "Sorry, could you repeat the question, please?"
  • Need a moment: "That is a good question. Let me think for a second."
  • Do not know: "I am not sure yet. I would check it by ..." (then say how).
  • Want to check you understood: "Do you mean ...?"

When they ask "Do you have any questions for us?":

Yes, thank you. I read that the Information Science IPGP scholarship accepts applications until 13 November, for October 2027 entry. If you think my application is suitable, would it be possible to apply with the lab's support?

Second question, only if there is time:

Is there anything you would recommend I read or build before I start, so that I can be useful to the lab from the first month?

Closing:

Thank you very much for your time today, Dr. Ji and Dr. Reid. I really enjoyed talking with you, and I look forward to hearing from you.

[Wait for them to end the call, or say "Goodbye" and leave after they do.]
