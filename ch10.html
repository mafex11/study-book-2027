Part 1: Before Monday (23 to 26 Oct)

Chapter 10 of 30

Weak points and honest answers

When and how long: Sunday, about 45 minutes. One honest line each for: Japanese level, the IJIRAE paper vs "no publications", "most of my software with coding agents", using AI on the proposal, and statistics (#28).

What this file is: every weak point an interviewer may attack, with:

  • They may ask: the likely question.
  • Your answer: a short, honest model answer in your voice.
  • Do not say: what would hurt you.

All facts about papers were checked in the PDFs in ../04_Papers/ on 21 Oct 2026. The personal answers come from your answer sheet (/Users/mafex/code/personal/masters-research/naist/answer_sheet.md); say a line that depends on its CONFIRM list (section C) only once it is true. Change the wording so it sounds like you. Do not memorise.

Long answers are split. Say this is the 20 to 40 second answer. Only if they ask more is backup. Do not say the backup unless they ask.

How to answer any attack (use this pattern every time)

  1. Agree with the true part. "Yes, that is a real limit."
  2. Say what you will do about it. One or two concrete steps.
  3. Say what you will not claim. "So I will report it as an association", or "So my results only cover this kind of rule."

Short is better. If you do not know, say: "I do not know yet. I would check it in the pilot" or "I would ask my supervisor. My first idea is ...".

A. Novelty and related work

1. "What exactly is new?"

They may ask: "Many papers already study context files. What is new in yours?"

Your answer:

"Each piece exists somewhere. What I did not find is all of them together. I take one real rule from a file a developer wrote. I remove it or reword it while the rest of the file stays the same. I run two production agents on real tasks from the same repository. And I measure with scripts both whether the rule was followed and how the code changed. Guardrails removed single rules but measured only pass rate. Harness-IF scored single rules, but on tasks written for the benchmark. I say 'as far as I know', because the field moves fast."

Do not say:

  • "Nobody has studied this." (Many have studied parts of it.)
  • "My study is the first." (You cannot prove that.)
  • "Previous studies are wrong."

2. "Gloaguen et al. [5] already showed agents follow instructions."

They may ask: "Gloaguen say instructions in context files are well followed. Why study it again?"

Your answer:

"They showed it mainly from traces of tool use. For example, uv was used 1.6 times per task when the file mentioned it, and almost never when it did not. They did not remove single rules, and they did not check rules about the code itself, like where tests go. Their category ablation used only LLM-generated files. Also, following a rule is not the same as the rule being effective. The agent may do it anyway. That is why I compare with the rule removed. Gloaguen also say context files are useful for 'non-standard coding practices'. Those are exactly the rules I test."

Do not say:

  • "Their study is weak." (It is careful and well known.)
  • "They say context files are useless." (They say files do not generally raise task success, and cost over 20% more. Developer files gave +2.4%, p = 0.21, not significant.)

3. "Guardrails [6] already did a per-rule ablation."

They may ask: "Guardrails removed each of 18 rules one by one. Isn't that your study?"

Your answer:

Say this: "It is the closest design, and it inspired my RQ2 about wording. But their rules were general rules they curated, not rules from each repository's own file, and they measured only whether tests passed, with one agent and one model."

Only if they ask more: "They used Claude Code with Opus 4.6 on SWE-bench Verified. I use each project's own rules, I measure compliance and code quality, and I use two agents. Also, they say their only significant single rule would not survive correction for multiple tests, so the 'negative rules help' result is a hint I want to test with compliance."

Do not say:

  • "Guardrails proved negative rules work." (They call it suggestive, not conclusive.)

4. "Harness-IF [10] already scores single rules against a withheld baseline."

They may ask: "Harness-IF does exactly that: single rules, and runs with the rule withheld."

Your answer:

"Yes, and I use the same idea: compare with the rule withheld. The differences are the material and the measurement. Their items were written by the authors, 'project-authored rather than direct copies of source repositories'. Mine are real tasks from the repository the rule came from. And 86.8% of their verdicts involve an LLM judge, which they call the dominant uncertainty. I use scripts. I also measure code quality, which they do not."

Do not say:

  • "Harness-IF is synthetic so it does not count." (It is careful work; just say how yours differs.)

5. "Agent Skill Evolution [11] already showed adding a checkable rule changes behaviour."

They may ask: "Wang et al. added and removed checkable rules and replayed them. What is different?"

Your answer:

"They studied SKILL.md files, not CLAUDE.md or AGENTS.md. Their agent runs used one research scaffold, mini-SWE-agent, not production agents. Their finding, that rules naming a command or path work best, is the reason I test specificity in RQ2. So I build on it."

Do not say:

  • "They studied the same files." (They did not.)

6. "Cai et al. [12] already showed compliance goes up after rule updates."

They may ask: "Cai found compliance rose from 49% to 72% after rule updates. Isn't that your real-PR study?"

Your answer:

"It is related. Their rise was judged by an LLM, Gemini 3 Flash Preview. They could not separate code written by agents from code written by people, and they say they do not aim to establish causality. My real-PR part looks only at agent pull requests, uses human pull requests in the same repositories as a comparison, and uses script checks. I still report it as association."

Do not say:

  • "Cai showed rules cause a 23% improvement." (It is 22.99 percentage points, judged by an LLM, with causality disclaimed.)

7. "Voria, Kashiwa et al. [9] already plan to link context files to code quality."

They may ask: (likely from Kashiwa) "How is this different from our registered report?"

Your answer:

"Your registered report looks at changes to the whole file, classified into types like corrective or adaptive, and relates them to the quality of later agent commits across many repositories. It reports associations. My study works at the level of one rule, in a controlled experiment, and measures compliance with that rule as well as quality. I think they fit together. Your mining shows what happens in real projects. My experiment can say what one rule causes. For example, I could check whether rules that were added as corrective changes are more effective."

Background facts (checked): [9] is a Stage 1 registered report at ICSME 2026, with no results yet. It plans structural quality as the change in cyclomatic complexity, lines of code and coupling between each agent commit and its parent, plus Corrective Commit Probability, with Kruskal-Wallis tests. It states a threat "limiting causal interpretation" and frames results "as associations rather than causation".

Do not say:

  • "Voria et al. found ..." (There are no results yet.)
  • "It is an Iida paper." (Iida is not an author. Kashiwa is.)
  • Anything that sounds like competing with them.

8. "Our PROFES paper [1] already listed controlled experiments as future work."

They may ask: "Isn't this just the future work section of our PROFES paper?"

Your answer:

"I read that section. I came to this question from my own work, where some rules had no effect. I test single real rules one at a time and measure both compliance and code quality."

Do not say:

  • "Yes." (It concedes that your plan is just their future work.)
  • "Your lab did not do it, so I will", or that the lab's papers have not run it yet. (Sounds like criticism, and the lab may have unpublished work on it.)
  • "I got the idea from your paper." (Only if that is true. The honest story is that you came to it from practice.)
  • Anything about this future work section before they raise it.

9. "McMillan [7] found structure has no effect. Why expect anything from one rule?"

They may ask: "If size and position do not matter, maybe single rules do not matter either."

Your answer:

"McMillan varied structure, not content, and used one made-up rule, adding a // @tracked comment. The no-file baseline had 0 compliant functions out of 524, so that rule is followed only because it is written. Real rules are different. Many match what agents do anyway. He also says he did not vary phrasing. So the content and wording of real rules is still open. If I find that many rules have no effect, that is useful too: it tells developers which rules they can delete."

Do not say:

  • "McMillan showed structure does not matter." (Only for one synthetic rule; two of his nulls have Bayes factor support, two do not.)

10. "Someone may publish this before you."

They may ask: "The field moves fast. What if this is published next month?"

Your answer:

"That is a real risk. New papers came out even in October 2026. I would check arXiv regularly. If a close study appears, I would build on it, for example by testing its findings on real rules and code quality, or by focusing on the real pull request part. The rule checkers and dataset would still be useful."

Do not say:

  • "Nobody else is working on this."

B. Design and measurement

11. Only script-checkable rules (generalization)

They may ask: "Most important rules are about design or process. Your results only cover easy rules."

Your answer:

"Yes. I say this in the proposal: the results speak for rules a script can check, not for design or process rules. I chose them because I can measure them the same way every time, without a judge. They are not rare: style, testing and tooling rules are common in real files. Later, I could add some design rules with an LLM judge plus a human check, but I would report them separately."

Do not say:

  • "My results will apply to all rules."
  • "Design rules are not important."

12. Rules a linter already enforces

They may ask: "If a linter already enforces a rule, why study it? The linter will fix it anyway."

Your answer:

"In my runs, the agent does not get the linter output unless it runs the linter itself, so I can see what the rule alone does. Linter coverage is one of my three properties in RQ2. If linter-covered rules turn out less effective, that supports the 'Lint Leakage' smell in [8], which they found in 62% of files, and my checker can tell developers to remove those rules. If they are more effective, that is interesting too."

Do not say:

  • "Linters do not matter."

13. Circularity between compliance and quality

They may ask: "If the rule is about style and you measure style warnings, you measure the same thing twice."

Your answer:

"Yes, that is why RQ3 leaves out any check that encodes the rule. If the rule is 'lines under 100 characters', I drop the line-length warning from the quality count. Some rules overlap with a metric in a softer way, for example 'keep functions small' and complexity. I will mark those rules and report RQ3 with and without them. Also, for RQ3 I compare file versions, not runs that happened to comply, so there is no selection effect."

Do not say:

  • "There is no circularity." (Admit the soft overlap.)

14. Keeping the meaning when rewording

They may ask: "How do you know 'put tests in tests/' and 'do not put tests outside tests/' mean the same thing?"

Your answer:

"I use a fixed template that only flips the polarity, and a second person checks each pair. If a clean flip is not possible without changing the meaning, I do not reword that rule. For example 'do not use print' cannot simply become 'use X' without adding information. I will report how many rules could be reworded, and the agreement between me and the second person."

Do not say:

  • "Meaning is always preserved." (It is not, for some rules.)

15. Claude Code reads CLAUDE.md, Codex reads AGENTS.md

They may ask: "The two agents get the text in different files and in different ways. Is that a fair comparison?"

Your answer:

"The words are identical, only the file name differs, because that is the file each agent reads. The tools also deliver the file differently: Claude Code's documentation says CLAUDE.md is added as a message after the system prompt. So when I compare agents, I compare the whole product, model plus harness plus how it reads the file, the way developers use them. I do not claim anything about the models alone. I also make sure only one file exists in each run, because newer Claude Code versions also read AGENTS.md when there is no CLAUDE.md."

Do not say:

  • "Claude is better than GPT at following rules." (You measure agents, not models.)

16. The repository already shows the convention, or the rule is also in the README

They may ask: "If all existing tests are in tests/, the agent copies that. Removing the rule will not change anything."

Your answer:

"Yes, that can happen, and it is a real finding: the rule is followed but not effective. The no-file version shows this default. I will also record whether the same rule appears in the README or CONTRIBUTING file, because Gloaguen found that LLM-generated context files mostly repeat the documentation. I can use that as a rule property, and I will report it."

Do not say:

  • "Agents only learn rules from the context file."

17. Rules interact with each other

They may ask: "If you remove one rule, another rule may still imply it, like 'follow PEP 8' implies line length."

Your answer:

"Good point. When I select rules, I will mark rules that overlap with another rule in the same file, and either avoid them or report them separately. The neutral sentence version also keeps the length and position the same, so a change is more likely due to the rule's meaning."

18. Is the checker accurate? Why only 20 examples?

They may ask: "Your measurement depends on your scripts. How do you know they are right?"

Your answer:

"I check each script by hand on at least 20 real examples and report how many it got right. Twenty is a minimum, not a magic number. If I find an error, I fix the script and check more. I also keep the agent's diffs, so anyone can recheck."

19. "Why not use an LLM judge and cover more rules?"

They may ask: "You use LLM judges in your job. Why not here?"

Your answer:

"An LLM judge would cover more rules, but it adds its own error. In Harness-IF, 86.8% of verdicts used a judge, and the authors say verdicts were 'far less stable' when they swapped the judge, with 62.1% agreement. For the core study I want a measurement that gives the same answer every time. I could add a judged set later, checked by a human, and keep it separate."

20. Headless, single-turn runs are not how developers really use agents

They may ask: "Real developers talk to the agent many times. Your runs are one prompt."

Your answer:

"True. My setting is closer to agents that open pull requests on their own, which is what AIDev records. McMillan also notes multi-turn sessions may behave differently. I will state it as a limit, and the real pull request study covers real use."

21. The task may not trigger the rule

They may ask: "What if the agent writes no tests? Then you cannot check a test rule."

Your answer:

"I pair each rule with tasks where it applies, and I report runs with no applicable place separately. If removing a rule changes whether the agent writes tests at all, that is also an effect, and I will report it."

22. The agent could find the real solution online

They may ask: "Agents can browse. What if it looks up the merged pull request?"

Your answer:

"I remove the git history and remotes, like Gloaguen did. I would also turn off web tools where the agent allows it, and search the traces for visits to the project's pull requests. Gloaguen checked manually and found no cheating."

23. Python only

They may ask: "Why only Python? Results may differ in other languages."

Your answer:

"Python has good tools and a precedent in Gloaguen's benchmark. It is a limit. Gloaguen even note that Python is well represented in training data, so context files may matter less there. If the method works, another language is a natural next step."

24. Are complexity and warnings really "quality" or "maintainability"?

They may ask: "Your pre-application text says maintainability. You measure complexity and warnings. Are those maintainability?"

Your answer:

"They are common proxies, not the same thing. The lab's readability paper also used radon complexity and Maintainability Index. In the real pull request study I count later fix commits, like Sawada et al., which is closer to real maintenance, but still a proxy. Sawada et al. themselves warn that low maintenance frequency alone does not mean high quality. I will call them proxies."

C. Statistics

25. Statistical power with 3 runs

They may ask: "Three runs per version is very few. How can you detect anything?"

Your answer:

Say this: "For one rule on its own, three runs on two tasks is not enough, and I will not make strong claims per rule. The main test for RQ1 pools all rules, around 180 removed-rule runs per agent, compared with the original file on the same tasks."

Only if they ask more: "The mixed model uses all of them. Run-to-run noise is real: in Guardrails, a solved task flipped about 12% of the time on a rerun. After the pilot, I will use the variance I see to simulate the study and choose the number of tasks and runs. If I need more power, I would add tasks rather than repeats."

Do not say:

  • "Three runs is enough." (You do not know yet.)
  • Exact power numbers you have not calculated.

26. Many rules means many tests

They may ask: "With 30 rules, some will look significant by chance."

Your answer:

"Yes. The main result is the pooled effect. For per-rule results I will show intervals and correct for multiple tests, for example with Holm or Benjamini-Hochberg. Guardrails is a good warning: their one significant rule would not survive correction."

27. "What model do you use for complexity and warnings? And should the effect differ by rule or agent?"

They may ask: (likely Ji) "Logistic regression works for yes/no. Complexity is not yes/no. And the removal effect may differ by rule."

Your answer:

Say this: "Right. For compliance, I model followed places out of applicable places per run, a binomial mixed model. For complexity I would use a linear mixed model on the change in complexity, and for warning counts a count model like Poisson or negative binomial, with the same fixed and random effects."

Only if they ask more: "The removal effect probably differs by rule, so I would try a random slope for version by rule, and an interaction between version and agent. For RQ2, a rule property has to interact with the file version, because the question is whether removing, say, a linter-covered rule changes compliance less than removing other rules. I would decide the final model with my supervisor and fix it before the main runs."

Do not say:

  • "I am an expert in mixed models." (Say you are learning.)

28. You have no statistics course

They may ask: "Have you studied statistics?"

Your answer:

"At VTU, probability and basic statistical inference were part of my third-semester mathematics course, Mathematics for Computer Science. It covered probability distributions, sampling, hypothesis tests such as the t-test and chi-square test, and basic ANOVA. I have not taken a full statistics course, which is why statistics is in my Year 1 plan. I understand the basic ideas I use: proportions, logistic regression, why repeated runs are not independent, and why association is not causation. For this plan I studied logistic regression and mixed effects models on my own. I know I need to learn more, and the lab's experience is one reason I want to study here."

Do not say:

  • That you have taken a course you have not taken.

D. Practical risks

29. Training data contamination

They may ask: "These are public pull requests. The model may have seen the answer."

Your answer:

Say this: "Yes. I prefer pull requests merged after the models' training cutoff, and I report older and newer tasks separately. Every file version of a task faces the same contamination, so it should not create a difference between versions by itself, though it could make differences smaller."

Only if they ask more: "AIDev's paper version stops in August 2025, so I would take newer pull requests from the same repositories directly from GitHub. Gloaguen split their tasks by cutoff and found no significant or consistent difference, but I would still check. If the model just remembers the merged code, differences shrink, which is another reason to compare old and new tasks."

Do not say:

  • "Contamination is not a problem."

30. Version drift and reproducibility

They may ask: "Agents update every few weeks. How can anyone reproduce this?"

Your answer:

"I pin the agent version and the full model ID, run in a fresh container, and log everything for each run: versions, model IDs, file hashes, tokens and cost, like McMillan did. I run all versions in a random, mixed order in a short time window, so an API change hits all versions equally. I also isolate user settings: no personal CLAUDE.md, memory or global AGENTS.md, and I test this with a canary instruction. Exact numbers will not repeat forever, because models are retired. So I release all scripts, file versions and tasks, so others can rerun with new models."

Do not say:

  • "The results will hold for all future models."

31. Cost and who pays

They may ask: "About 1,300 to 1,900 dollars. Who pays?"

Your answer:

Say this: "It is an estimate from Gloaguen's per-task costs. I do not know yet who would pay. I would ask whether it fits a lab project budget, and if it does not, I can make it smaller: fewer runs, a cheaper model, or an open-weight agent on NAIST's own computers for part of the study."

Only if they ask more: "Their costs were 1.15 to 1.51 dollars for Claude Code and 0.32 to 0.57 for Codex, times 900 runs each. The model choice matters a lot: Guardrails reported about 5 dollars per task with Opus 4.6. I would set a budget cap per run. I can pay for the two-repository pilot myself; it is a few hundred US dollars. With about 6 rules, 2 tasks each, 5 file versions and 3 runs, that is 180 runs per agent, 360 in total. At the per-task costs in Gloaguen et al., that is about 207 to 272 dollars for Claude Code and 58 to 103 dollars for Codex, so about 265 to 375 dollars in total."

Do not say:

  • "The lab will pay." (Do not assume.)
  • "It will definitely cost X." (It is an estimate from another study's tasks.)

32. Building runnable task environments is hard

They may ask: "SWE-bench style environments take a lot of work. And many pull requests have no tests."

Your answer:

Say this: "Yes, this is the hardest practical part. So I start with repositories whose tests already run in CI, and the pilot on two repositories counts how many usable tasks I can get before I commit to anything."

Only if they ask more: "Gloaguen's benchmark has 138 tasks from 5,694 pull requests in 12 repositories, so the yield is low, about 2 in 100. Pull requests in smaller repositories often had no tests, so they generated tests with an LLM and reviewed them by hand. If there are too few fail-before, pass-after pull requests, I can do the same, and report it."

Do not say:

  • "It is easy, I do Docker at work." (Docker experience helps, but say it is hard.)

33. What if you find nothing?

They may ask: "What if removing rules changes nothing?"

Your answer:

"Then that is a result, if the study has enough power to see a meaningful effect. It would say many real rules are followed only because agents do it anyway, so developers could shorten their files. That fits the cost increase Gloaguen found. The dataset and checkers would still be useful."

34. Is two years enough? What do you cut?

They may ask: "Courses, experiment, mining study, tool, thesis. In two years?"

Your answer:

"It is tight. RQ1 and RQ2 are the core. If time runs short, I shrink the real pull request study first, for example to one or two rule types. The tool is small and reuses the smell detectors of [8]."

35. "A few developers" is a weak tool evaluation

They may ask: "Evaluating the checker with a few developers is not much."

Your answer:

"Yes, it is a first, small evaluation: do the warnings make sense and are they useful. I could ask lab members and maintainers of the projects I study. A bigger evaluation would be future work."

E. The real pull request study: confounds

36. Rules are added after violations (regression to the mean)

They may ask: "Developers add a rule right after the agent breaks it. Things would improve anyway."

Your answer:

"Yes. Cai's survey found 77.78% of developers change rules mainly to correct AI errors. So rule dates are not random, and some improvement would happen anyway. That is why I use human pull requests in the same repositories as a comparison, look at a longer period before the rule, and report the results as associations. The causal claim comes from the controlled experiment, not this part."

37. Agents improve over time

They may ask: "Models get better every few months. Compliance may rise for that reason."

Your answer:

"Right. Human pull requests in the same repository and the same months help, because they share the time trend. I can also compare against repositories that did not add the rule in the same period. It is still association."

38. Linter changes at the same time

They may ask: "Maybe the project added a linter rule at the same time."

Your answer:

"I will check the linter and formatter config files around the rule date and leave out cases where they changed."

39. Telling agent work from human work

They may ask: "How do you know which pull requests are by agents? And where do the human pull requests come from?"

Your answer:

"The AIDev paper describes agent pull requests. For human pull requests in the same repositories I would use the GitHub API, or AIDev's human table where it covers my repositories. Labels are best effort. People can hide the agent trailer, and Sawada et al. had to exclude Codex because it does not appear as a commit owner. So I treat 'human' as 'not marked as agent', check a sample by hand, and say it in threats to validity."

Background fact (checked on the AIDev Hugging Face page, 21 Oct 2026): the v3 release (932,791 agent pull requests, the version in the MSR 2026 paper) also has human_pull_request and human_pr_task_type tables, sampled from the same repositories but only from those with more than 500 stars. The current main release (v4, about 2.7 million pull requests) does not list a human table.

Do not say:

  • "AIDev labels are perfect."
  • "AIDev has no human pull requests."

40. Is there even enough data?

They may ask: "How many repositories add a checkable rule and have enough agent pull requests before and after?"

Your answer:

"I do not know yet. That is why my first step is to count. Agent READMEs had only two repositories in its indentation case study. If there are few, I focus on rule types that recur, like test location or logging."

F. About you

41. "Your BrandMemory A/B test is only one comparison."

They may ask: "Contractions from 6 to 0 on one demo. That is not evidence."

Your answer:

"I agree, and I say so in the proposal. It is one comparison, not a study. What I learned is to measure instead of assuming. It also showed me two ways rules fail: they get cut off, or another instruction overrides them. That is exactly why I want to do a proper experiment."

Do not say:

  • "It proved that rules work."
  • Details you do not remember. If asked how long the demo text was: "I do not remember the exact word count."

How you ran it, in two sentences: "I created the same product demo twice on our beta system, in the same workspace, from the same source video and with the same request, 'Announce a new feature to customers': once with brand memory turned off and once with it on. Then I counted contractions and the words 'you' and 'your' in the text of both demos."

42. The Japanese line on your CV

They may ask: "Your CV says Japanese, limited working. Can you work in Japanese?"

Your answer (better to say it before they ask, for example when languages come up):

"I need to correct one thing on the CV I sent. It says my Japanese is 'Limited Working'. That is a mistake. I am a beginner, below N5. I have started studying with the Genki I textbook and a daily app, about 30 minutes a day, and I want to reach N5 before I arrive and continue with NAIST's Japanese classes. I am sorry for the mistake. I can send a corrected CV."

Do not say:

  • Anything that suggests you can work or study in Japanese now.
  • A long story about how the mistake happened.

43. The IJIRAE paper, and "I have not yet published academic papers"

They may ask: "Your CV lists a 2023 IJIRAE paper with a best paper award, but your pre-application text says you have not published academic papers. Which is it?"

Your answer:

"That line in my pre-application text was not precise. I have one 2023 paper from my B.E., a review paper in a special issue of IJIRAE, and I was the second of four authors. It has no experiments of our own, and it is not in software engineering. I have no research publication in software engineering."
