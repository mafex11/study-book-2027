Part 1: Before Monday (23 to 26 Oct)

Chapter 12 of 30

Questions to ask the lab

When and how long: Sunday, about 15 minutes: your one question for them. If you chose October, ask about the IPGP scholarship (deadline 13 Nov).

What this file is: 10 thoughtful questions for the end of the lab interview, who to ask, why each one is good, and what to listen for. Plus what not to ask.

How to use it:

  • You will usually get time for 2 or 3 questions. Pick them before the call and write them on your index card.
  • Always include one practical question (number 10) and one research question.
  • Ask each question to the person whose work it fits. Name their paper only if you have read it.
  • Listen to the answer, say thank you, and write it down. Do not argue with the answer.
  • If they already answered a question during the interview, do not ask it again. Say instead: "You already answered my question about X. Thank you."

Facts about papers and people below come from ../07_Lab_and_People/lab_profile_and_people.md and the PDFs in ../04_Papers/, checked on 21 Oct 2026.

The 10 questions

1. Advice before the official exam (anyone; ask this first)

"Is there anything in my plan that you think I should change, or any paper I should read, before I write the final proposal for the official exam?"

  • Why it is good: shows you are coachable, and gets you their advice in their own words. The lab site says "multiple faculty members spend time working with" students to choose a direction [V].
  • Why it matters for you: past applicants report that papers and questions from their lab meetings came back in the official exam [A].
  • Listen for: paper titles, a part of the plan they think is weak, a part they like. Write them down. Read the papers within a week.

2. Overlap with work that is not public yet (Dr. Kashiwa or Dr. Reid)

"Your website says the newest research is often not public yet. Is the lab already working on something close to my plan, so that I can build on it instead of repeating it?"

  • Why it is good: the lab site says the latest research "is often not posted here because it cannot be made public on the website" [V]. You avoid duplicating their work, and you show respect for it.
  • Listen for: a current student's topic you could connect to; data or tools you could reuse.

3. Causal claims for rule properties (Dr. Ji)

"In RQ2, wording is the only property I can change directly. Specificity and linter coverage differ between rules, so I plan to report them as associations. Is there a design you would recommend to get closer to a causal answer for those, or is it better to keep them as associations?"

  • Why it is good: Dr. Ji works on causality and LLM-based agents [V], and co-taught an ASE 2025 tutorial on causal inference for software engineering [V]. The question shows you already know the limit of your own design.
  • Listen for: ideas like matching rules, or rewriting a vague rule into a specific one as a second manipulation. Do not promise to adopt them on the spot. Say "Thank you, I will think about how to fit that in."

4. Interactions between rules (Dr. Ji)

"In the CAM paper, you point out that removing one part at a time can miss effects that are not additive. My design removes one rule at a time. With a fixed budget, what would be a sensible way to check for interactions between rules?"

  • Why it is good: the CAM paper (ISSTA 2026, Dr. Ji as co-corresponding author) criticises "all-but-one ablation designs" and says the parts' "collective effects on downstream performance are non-additive" (local PDF L12). This is a real limit of your plan, and you raise it yourself.
  • Only ask if: you have read the CAM abstract and introduction. Say "the CAM paper" or "your group's CAM paper". It is HKUST-led, so do not call it SD lab work.
  • Listen for: a small extension, for example testing a few rule pairs in the pilot.

5. Evaluating the checker tool (Dr. Reid)

"For NCQ you ran two user studies with 20 participants in total. For a first evaluation of a small checker in a master's project, what size and design would you suggest? And how do students in the lab usually recruit developers?"

  • Why it is good: Dr. Reid builds tools and tests them with developers. The NCQ paper (IEEE TSE 2023) reports "two user studies with a total of 20 participants" (arXiv 2101.00756 abstract). Your Year 2 plan is exactly this kind of work.
  • Listen for: a realistic number, and whether lab members or open source maintainers are a normal pool.

6. Fit with the registered report and the FOREST project (Dr. Kashiwa)

"When the second stage of the Voria et al. study runs, could a per-rule experiment like mine be useful next to it, for example to check whether a type of change really affects quality?"

  • Why it is good: the Voria et al. registered report (ICSME 2026, with Dr. Kashiwa) says that because context files "contain heterogeneous instructions and modifications may affect only parts of the file", this is "limiting causal interpretation", and it frames results "as associations rather than causation" (local PDF L9). Your design works at the level of one rule. The word to use is "complement", never "fix".
  • Alternative if FOREST comes up: "For the FOREST project, are you planning shared infrastructure that students use together, for example containers for running agents on real repository tasks?"
  • Listen for: whether they see your work as part of a bigger lab effort. That is a strong sign of fit.

7. API cost for agent experiments (anyone)

"Agent experiments cost money for model APIs. My estimate is about 1,300 to 1,900 US dollars for the main runs. How do students in the lab usually handle this? Should I design part of the study so it can run with open-weight models on NAIST's computers?"

  • Why it is good: it shows you thought about feasibility, and you do not assume the lab will pay. The lab lists a shared cluster with "16 NVIDIA A100 GPUs" [V].
  • Do not ask "Will the lab pay for it?" directly.
  • Listen for: whether there is a budget, a cap, or a preferred model.

8. Starting early: pilot or online internship (anyone)

"Before I enroll, would it be useful for me to start the pilot on two repositories, or to join an online internship with the lab? I would like to use the time well."

  • Why it is good: the lab says internships of one week or longer are possible, and online internships are possible [V]. It shows commitment without asking for anything big.
  • Listen for: a yes, a contact person, or a suggestion of what to read or build first.
  • Be careful: do not promise hours you do not have while you work full time. If they ask how much time you can give, say: "About 5 hours a week for lab work while I work full time, and more after I leave Layerpath."

9. Lab life for an international student (anyone)

"How do international students usually take part in lab meetings and seminars? And is there anything you would recommend for my Japanese before I arrive?"

  • Why it is good: it is a real, practical question, and it shows you take Japanese seriously as a beginner. The lab site is bilingual and says English communication is "essential" [V], but the language of lab meetings is not stated anywhere [UNVERIFIED].
  • Do not assume English is the working language, and do not ask in a way that sounds like a complaint.
  • Listen for: how meetings work, and any advice on Japanese courses.

10. Start date and scholarship (anyone; ask even if time is short)

"Would you advise April or October 2027 entry for me? And if October fits, would the lab consider supporting an application for the MEXT scholarship through NAIST's International Priority Graduate Program? I understand the deadline is 13 November."

  • Why it is good: it is time-critical. The IPGP application window is 14 Oct to 13 Nov 2026, for October 2027 entry only, with 4 master's places in the division, and you apply through the prospective supervisor, who must write a Letter of Acceptance [V, 00_intel_report.md section 5.3].
  • Only ask the second half if: you decided you want October 2027 and you can get the Dean's recommendation and department evaluation report in time. Your decision is October 2027, so ask this question in full, both halves.
  • If you already asked about IPGP in the scheduling email, ask only the first half, or: "Thank you for considering my question about the IPGP scholarship. Is there anything I should prepare?"
  • Listen for: their preference for your start date, and exactly what they need from you and by when.

If Prof. Iida joins: one extra question

"I read that your 2010 to 2012 project studied the relation between the degree of process observance and software quality. Is there a lesson from that work that I should keep in mind when I relate rule compliance to code quality?"

  • Why it is good: it links your RQ3 to his own long research line. The final summary of his KAKEN grant 22500027 lists "the statistical analysis about the relation between the degree of process observance and software quality" [V, ../07_Lab_and_People/lab_profile_and_people.md section 2.1].
  • Only ask if: you can say the project in your own words.

Backup question (Dr. Reid or Dr. Kashiwa)

"In the Agent READMEs study you used a taxonomy of 16 categories. Would you recommend that I group my rules with it, or is a simpler grouping better for rules a script can check?"

  • Why it is good: it uses their own paper in your design (the paper reports "a final taxonomy of 16 categories", local PDF L2).

Which to pick

Time left Ask
1 minute 10 (first half, or both halves if you decided on October)
3 minutes 1 and 10
5 minutes or more 1, one question for each interviewer present (3 or 4 for Dr. Ji, 5 for Dr. Reid, 6 for Dr. Kashiwa), and 10
They ask "anything else?" again 8 or 9

What not to ask

  • "Will I be accepted?" or "How did I do?" Ask "What is the next step?" instead.
  • Anything already on the lab website (number of students, facilities, graduate careers). It shows you did not read it.
  • "Can I keep my full-time job during the master's?" as an open question. Decided: you will leave Layerpath before you enrol, so NAIST needs no employer documents. Do not mention a notice period.
  • Questions about salary, RA pay amounts or stipends, unless they raise funding first.
  • "What should I research?" You have a plan. Ask how to improve it (question 1).
  • Questions that criticise their papers, for example "Why didn't your paper run a controlled experiment?"
  • Anything about anyone's nationality or personal background.
