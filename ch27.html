Chapter 27 of 30

Self-introductions, research pitches and slides

When and how long: February to 10 March, step 5: build a 3-minute research summary with no notes and no slides (the exam allows neither). Connection test on 8 March, exam on 10 March.

What this file is: spoken scripts in your voice, consistent with the proposal you sent (.context/proposal_text.md) and your 10 Oct pre-application text.

  • Self-introductions: 30 seconds, 1 minute, 3 minutes.
  • Research pitches: 2 minutes, 5 minutes.
  • A 6-slide deck with exact slide text and speaker notes.

How to use the scripts:

  • Do not memorise them word for word. Learn the order of the ideas, then say it in your own words. If an interviewer interrupts, answer them, then continue or stop.
  • Speak slowly. The word counts assume about 120 to 130 words a minute, a calm pace for video. Time yourself with a phone. If you run over, cut, do not speed up.
  • Every number here is from your proposal or the papers it cites. Do not add numbers.

Which one to use when:

They say Use
"Please introduce yourself briefly" / very short call 30 second intro
"Please introduce yourself" (default) 1 minute intro
"Tell us about yourself and your background" with time 3 minute intro
"Tell us about your research plan" (default) 2 minute pitch
"Please explain your proposal" with time given, or "Can you present it?" 5 minute pitch, or the slides if they invite screen sharing
Official exam in March: "Explain your essay in 3 minutes" (common, [A]) Cut the 5 minute pitch down, or stretch the 2 minute one. No slides or notes are allowed there [V]

4. Research pitch: 2 minutes (about 275 words)

My question is: which rules in a context file do coding agents follow, and does a rule change the quality of their code?

Developers steer agents like Claude Code and Codex with files such as CLAUDE.md and AGENTS.md. Your lab's papers show what developers write in these files. Other studies found that a whole file did not generally raise task success, and added over 20% to cost. One study removed single rules, but measured only whether tests passed. Another checked single rules, but on tasks written for a benchmark. As far as I know, no study has yet removed or reworded one real rule at a time, on real tasks from the same repository, and measured with scripts both whether agents follow it and how the code changes.

I have three questions. First, does removing one real rule change how often agents follow it? Second, which properties make a rule effective: negative or positive wording, specificity, and whether a linter already enforces it? Third, does keeping a rule change code quality, such as tests, complexity and warnings?

The main study is a controlled experiment. I take about 10 Python repositories from AIDev and about 30 rules that a script can check. The tasks are replayed merged pull requests. Each task runs with five versions of the file: original, rule removed, rule reworded, rule replaced by a neutral sentence, and no file. Claude Code and Codex run each version three times in a clean container. That is at most 1,800 runs, after a pilot on two repositories.

I expect a public dataset, evidence on which kinds of rules work, and a small checker tool for developers.

If they want 1 minute: say the first paragraph, the gap sentence ("As far as I know..."), the three questions in one line each, and "a controlled experiment with five file versions, two agents and three runs".

5. Research pitch: 5 minutes (about 730 words)

At a calm pace of 120 to 130 words a minute this is about 6 minutes. If you must fit exactly 5 minutes, cut about 80 words, starting with the Background part.

Motivation (about 25 seconds)

At Layerpath I built BrandMemory, which gives a company's writing rules to a model as context. When I measured the output, some rules had no effect. Some were cut off at the end of a long prompt. Others were overridden by a default house style. After I fixed both, one small A/B test showed the rules working. That is one comparison, not a study. But it made me ask the same question about coding agents.

Background (about 35 seconds)

Coding agents now open many pull requests on GitHub. The AIDev dataset, in the version in its MSR paper, holds 932,791 pull requests from five agents. To steer agents, developers write context files such as CLAUDE.md and AGENTS.md. Your lab's studies show what these files contain, and that they grow through many small additions. Your EASE paper shows that humans do most of the later maintenance of agent code. So if an agent ignores a project's rules, a person pays for it later.

What is known, and the gap (about 60 seconds)

The results so far are mixed. Gloaguen and colleagues found that context files did not generally raise task success, and added over 20% to inference cost. They saw from traces that agents follow instructions, but did not test single rules. In "Guardrails Beat Guidance", random rules helped as much as expert rules. Its ablation of 18 rules found that the rules whose removal lowered the pass rate were "do not" rules, but it measured only task success. Harness-IF compares single rules with runs where the rule is withheld, but on tasks written for the benchmark. And a registered report by Voria and colleagues, including Dr. Kashiwa, plans to relate changes in context files to code quality, as associations.

As far as I know, no study has yet removed or reworded one real rule at a time in files written by developers, on real tasks from the same repositories, and measured with scripts both whether agents follow that rule and how the code changes. I call a rule effective if agents follow it more often when it is in the file than when it is removed.

Research questions (about 25 seconds)

RQ1: does removing one real rule change how often agents follow it? RQ2: which properties predict how effective a rule is: negative or positive wording, specificity, and whether the linter already enforces it? RQ3: does keeping a rule change code quality, measured by tests, complexity, and warnings that the rule itself does not check?

Design (about 70 seconds)

I start with about 10 Python repositories from AIDev that have a context file and tests that run in a container. From their files I take about 30 rules that a small script can check, like "put new tests in the tests folder". I check each script by hand on at least 20 examples. The tasks are merged pull requests whose own tests fail before and pass after. The agent starts from the code just before the pull request, with no git history, and the task does not mention the rule.

Each task runs with five versions of the file: the original, one rule removed, the rule reworded by a fixed template, the rule replaced by a neutral sentence of the same length, and no file. Claude Code and Codex run every version three times, with pinned versions, in a clean container. That is at most 1,800 runs, about 1,300 to 1,900 US dollars using the per-task costs in Gloaguen's paper. A pilot on two repositories sets the final numbers.

Analysis (about 25 seconds)

Compliance is the fraction of places in the new code that follow the rule. I use mixed effects logistic regression, with file version and agent as fixed effects, and task and rule as random effects. For RQ3 I compare file versions, not runs that happened to comply. Wording is tested directly. Specificity and linter coverage are associations.

Real pull requests and results (about 35 seconds)

A smaller second study looks at agent pull requests before and after a checkable rule was added, with human pull requests as a comparison, reported as associations. I expect three results: a public dataset of real, checkable rules and how often two agents follow them; evidence on which kinds of rules are effective; and in Year 2, a small checker that flags rules agents tend to ignore. I aim for MSR, ICSME or EASE.

Why this lab (about 15 seconds)

Your lab studies context files and agent code from the repository side. My study adds a controlled experiment on single rules. I would like to learn your methods for mining repositories and measuring quality. Thank you.

Notes:

  • If they ask "what was the A/B result?": contractions fell from 6 to 0, and "you" and "your" from 15 to 1.
  • If they stop you, stop. They may already know the background.
  • For the official exam in March (no slides, no notes), practise this from memory and cut it to 3 minutes: Motivation (2 sentences), gap (1 sentence), RQs, design (5 versions, 2 agents, 3 runs), expected results.

6. Slide deck: 6 slides

When to use it: only in the lab interview, and only if they invite screen sharing or ask you to present. Ask first: "I prepared six short slides. Would it be helpful if I share my screen?" If they say no, use the 2 minute or 5 minute pitch. Never in the official exam [V: no devices, slides or notes].

Design rules:

  • White background, black text, one accent colour at most. Works in black and white.
  • At least 24 pt for body text, 32 pt for titles. No animations.
  • Footer on every slide: Sudhanshu Pandit | Reception No. 2027M3055 | [slide number]/6
  • Talk to the camera, not the slide. About 40 to 60 seconds per slide, about 5 minutes in total.
  • Make a PDF copy too, in case the slide app fails while sharing.
